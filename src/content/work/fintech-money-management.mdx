## Executive summary

A consumer fintech app had solid transactional features — cards, transfers, statements — but
users opened it only when something demanded attention. The business wanted a money-management
layer that made the app worth checking proactively. This project designed that layer: a home
experience organised around the questions people actually ask about their money, with spending
insights that inform without moralising.

## Project context

The app served a young, salaried audience managing one main account. Competitors were shipping
budgeting features rapidly, and churn interviews kept surfacing the same theme: the app was a
utility, not a habit. The mandate was a money-management experience — the shape of it was open.

## Problem

The home screen was a reverse-chronological transaction feed. Functional, but it answered a
question nobody was asking. Research showed users open a banking app with one of three
questions, in descending frequency:

1. _"Am I okay?"_ — a two-second reassurance check
2. _"What was that?"_ — investigating a specific transaction
3. _"Can I afford this?"_ — a forward-looking spending decision

The feed served question 2, buried question 1, and ignored question 3 entirely.

## Users and audience

A two-week diary study (12 participants logging every money-check moment) revealed that most
app opens were **ambient checks** — under ten seconds, often in queues or before purchases. The
design consequence: the primary experience had to work at a glance, with depth available on
demand rather than by default.

  I open it, look at the number, feel either fine or a small dread, and close it. That's the whole
  visit.
</Quote>

## Business context

Habitual engagement was the strategic goal: users who check frequently hold higher balances and
adopt more products. But the team drew an explicit ethical line early — engagement through
usefulness, not through anxiety. No dark patterns, no manufactured urgency, no shame-based
"you spent too much" messaging. That constraint shaped the tone of every insight we shipped.

## Constraints

- **Data quality varied**: merchant categorisation was roughly 85% accurate, so any insight
  built on categories needed graceful handling of misclassification.
- **Regulatory review** required every numeric claim to be precise — no rounded "vibes".
- **Performance budget**: the home screen had to render its answer state instantly from cache.

## Role and responsibilities

I led the interaction and information design: the home-screen model, the spending-insight
system, data-visualisation patterns, prototype builds, and usability testing. A second designer
owned the visual language refresh that ran in parallel; we shared a weekly critique.

## Research

Alongside the diary study, we ran concept tests on three home-screen models with 15
participants, and reviewed twelve months of behavioural analytics with the data analyst to
validate that the diary study's "ambient check" pattern held at population scale (it did —
median session length was well under twenty seconds).

## Key findings

- Reassurance is the core job. Users wanted a **safe-to-spend figure** more than budgets:
  balance minus known upcoming commitments.
- **Budgets fail socially, not functionally.** Participants who'd abandoned budgeting features
  described guilt, not confusion. Insights had to be observational, not judgemental.
- Trust in insights is fragile: one wrong category ("that wasn't a restaurant") and users
  dismissed the whole system. Corrections had to be easy and visibly respected.

## Problem definition

**Design goal:** a home screen that answers "am I okay?" in under two seconds, and makes the
next two questions one tap away — while never making the user feel judged.

## User flows and information architecture

The redesigned IA inverted the hierarchy: the answer layer (safe-to-spend, upcoming
commitments) on top, the investigation layer (search-first transaction history) one tap deep,
and the planning layer (trends, recurring payments) as a distinct tab rather than interleaved
noise.

## Design exploration

Home-screen concepts tested: a **dashboard** (many widgets, user-configurable), a **feed with
smart cards** (insights interleaved with transactions), and the **answer-first** model. The
dashboard demoed well and tested badly — configuration was a chore nobody did. The smart-card
feed created exactly the anxiety the team had ruled out: insights interrupting a reassurance
check felt like ambush.

## Alternatives considered

We seriously considered shipping budgets because competitors had them. Concept testing killed
it as a launch feature: enthusiasm was high in the abstract and usage intent collapsed when
participants walked through their actual month. Safe-to-spend delivered the outcome budgets
promise (spending confidence) without the maintenance burden.

## Decisions and trade-offs

- **One number, honestly calculated.** Safe-to-spend only counts _known_ commitments and says
  so inline — over-promising precision would burn trust on the first surprise bill.
- **Insights are pull, mostly.** A single, dismissible insight card appears on the home screen
  at most weekly; everything else lives in the planning tab.
- **Category corrections teach the system visibly.** After a correction, the affected insight
  updates immediately with a "corrected" acknowledgement.

## Wireframes

Wireframes covered the answer state in its hard variants: healthy balance, tight balance,
overdrawn, payday, new account with no history, and stale data (offline). The tight-balance
state got the most iteration — it's where reassurance design either works or becomes spin.

## Prototyping

High-fidelity prototypes with real motion mattered here: the difference between a number that
feels stable and one that feels jumpy is timing. Number transitions were prototyped at multiple
speeds and tested — fast enough to feel current, slow enough not to alarm.

## Testing

Three rounds of moderated testing (18 sessions total) plus an unmoderated comprehension test on
the safe-to-spend calculation. The comprehension test was the critical one: if users couldn't
explain what the number meant, the feature was a liability. Iterated until explanations were
consistently accurate.

## Iterations

The safe-to-spend label went through four wordings. "Available" was dangerously wrong (it's a
banking term with a different meaning), "Spendable" tested as flippant, "Safe to spend" with a
one-line breakdown tested accurately — and the breakdown link ("balance − upcoming bills")
turned out to be the single most-tapped element in early sessions, so it was promoted from
fine print to a first-class disclosure.

## Final solution

A home screen led by the safe-to-spend figure with inline calculation disclosure; upcoming
commitments as a horizontally scannable strip; search-first transaction history one tap away;
and a planning tab with monthly trends that describe ("Groceries: ₹X this month, typical for
you") rather than judge.

## Design-system considerations

The data-visualisation patterns (trend bars, commitment strip, insight card) entered the design
system with explicit content guidelines — the "describe, don't judge" tone rules shipped as
documentation alongside the components, because the pattern's integrity depends on the words as
much as the pixels.

## Accessibility considerations

- All spending visualisations carry text equivalents; no information lives in colour alone.
- The safe-to-spend figure and its state changes are announced correctly by screen readers,
  including the disclosure breakdown.
- Touch targets meet platform minimums; the commitment strip scrolls with full keyboard and
  switch-control support on both platforms.
- Number-transition motion respects the reduce-motion system setting.

## Developer handoff

Specs documented every home-screen state with its data dependencies and fallbacks, plus a
decision log for the calculation display rules. I paired with the mobile engineers on the
number-transition implementation to keep the tested timing intact.

## Lessons learned

- **Design for the two-second visit.** Session length isn't a failure metric when the job is
  reassurance; optimising for "time in app" would have produced a worse product.
- **Tone is a design material.** The describe-don't-judge rule did more for trust than any
  visual decision.
- **Comprehension testing beats preference testing** for anything numeric — people prefer
  designs they misunderstand alarmingly often.

## What I'd do next

Extend the commitment model to detect irregular recurring payments (annual subscriptions are
the biggest safe-to-spend blind spot), and explore a shared-household view — the most requested
capability in every round of research.
