## Executive summary

A project-management SaaS was converting trial sign-ups poorly: teams signed up, poked around an
empty workspace, and left. This project reframed onboarding from _"finish configuring your
account"_ to _"experience the product's core value once, fast"_ — and rebuilt the first-session
flow around that goal. The redesign paired a shorter, role-aware setup with a pre-populated
sample project that let users feel the product working before investing any effort.

<Callout variant="insight">
  The core reframe: activation is a user outcome, not a checklist completion. Every screen in the
  first session either moves a new team toward their first real result or it's a candidate for
  removal.
</Callout>

## Project context

The product is a project-management tool for small agencies (5–50 people). Growth was
healthy at the top of the funnel — content marketing brought in steady trial sign-ups — but
paid conversion lagged behind benchmarks. Leadership's instinct was to add an interactive
product tour. The design team negotiated two weeks of discovery first to find out what was
actually failing.

## Problem

Funnel data showed the sharpest drop between account creation and second session: a large share
of trial teams never came back after day one. Support transcripts and session recordings
suggested new users hit an empty workspace, faced an eight-step configuration wizard written for
administrators, and gave up before seeing anything that resembled their work.

The working problem statement:

> New teams abandon the trial before experiencing the product's core value, because the first
> session demands configuration effort before demonstrating any payoff.

## Users and audience

Two distinct first-run audiences emerged from interviews, with conflicting needs:

- **The evaluator** — usually an operations lead or team manager on a deadline to pick a tool.
  Wants to judge fit in under 30 minutes. Configuration questions ("What's your team's billing
  cadence?") feel like obstacles.
- **The invited teammate** — arrives later via invitation into a half-configured workspace.
  Wants to find their tasks, not learn the tool's philosophy.

The legacy wizard served neither: it interrogated evaluators as if they were committed
administrators, and gave invited teammates the same generic tour as founders.

## Business context

Trial-to-paid conversion was the company's primary growth lever for the year. Even a modest
improvement in activation compounds: activated teams invite more members, and seat expansion was
the main revenue driver. This gave the project a clear success metric and — usefully — a clear
non-goal: this was not a visual refresh, and screens that worked would not be redesigned for
taste.

## Constraints

- **Six weeks** from kick-off to engineering handoff, fixed by the roadmap.
- **No backend changes** to the permissions model, which ruled out some ideal flows.
- **Existing design system** components had to be used wherever possible.
- **Sales-assisted accounts** shared the same entry flow and could not regress.

## Role and responsibilities

I owned the design work end-to-end: discovery interviews and funnel analysis with the PM,
journey mapping, flow redesign, UI design, prototype construction, moderated usability sessions,
and annotated handoff. Engineers joined weekly critiques from week one — several flow
simplifications came directly from their questions.

## Research

Discovery combined three evidence sources, chosen to triangulate cheaply within two weeks:

1. **Funnel analysis** of six months of trial cohorts, to locate _where_ users left.
2. **Eight interviews** — five with users who abandoned trials, three with recently
   converted admins — to understand _why_.
3. **Fifteen session recordings** of first runs, to see the moment-by-moment behaviour users
   couldn't accurately self-report.

<Quote attribution="Trial abandoner, interview 3 (illustrative)">
  I signed up to see if it would fit how we run client work. Twenty minutes in, I was still
  answering questions about time-zone defaults. I never saw a single project board.
</Quote>

## Key findings

- Users didn't need less onboarding — they needed **earlier proof**. Every abandoner
  interviewed said some version of "show me it working."
- The wizard's questions were **administrator questions**, but most first users were
  evaluators who didn't yet have the authority (or intent) to answer them.
- Empty states were the real onboarding surface. After the wizard, users landed on a blank
  dashboard whose only affordance was a "Create project" button with a 12-field form behind it.
- Invited teammates had the worst experience of all: the product assumed everyone was setting
  up a workspace, so a teammate's first session began with questions about company size.

## Problem definition

We turned the findings into one design goal with three measurable sub-goals:

**Design goal:** a new user in either audience reaches a populated, personally relevant project
view in their first session.

1. Time from sign-up to first project view: minutes, not sessions.
2. Setup questions asked before that moment: only those that change what the user sees.
3. Invited teammates get a distinct, shorter path.

## User flows and information architecture

The legacy flow was linear: eight wizard steps, then a blank dashboard. The redesign split it by
intent at the first screen — "Set up my team's workspace" versus "Just exploring" — and moved
every deferrable question into contextual moments inside the product.

## Design exploration

Three structural directions were explored at wireframe level:

- **A. Guided tour overlay** — leadership's original instinct. Cheapest, but tours explain
  value instead of demonstrating it, and our research said explanation wasn't the gap.
- **B. Template-first setup** — pick a template, get a scaffolded project. Better, but still
  asked users to do the work before feeling the payoff.
- **C. Sample-project-first** — drop every new user into a realistic, pre-populated sample
  project themed to their stated use case, with their real setup happening progressively.

## Alternatives considered

Direction A was prototyped anyway — deliberately — because it was the stakeholder favourite and
deserved a fair test rather than a designer veto. In testing, tour completion looked fine but
comprehension didn't: users dismissed the tour and then couldn't perform the tasks it had
described. Watching that footage together did more to align the team than any argument.

## Decisions and trade-offs

- **Sample data over real data.** A pre-populated project demonstrates value instantly but risks
  feeling like a demo. We mitigated with a persistent, dismissible banner: "This is a sample —
  start your own project when ready."
- **Two setup questions, not zero.** Role and use case materially change the sample project
  shown, so they stayed. Everything else moved into the product.
- **Deferred, not deleted.** Admin configuration still matters for converting accounts. It
  moved to a persistent, progress-tracked "Workspace setup" panel that admins could complete
  across sessions.

## Prototyping

A clickable Figma prototype covered the full evaluator journey (sign-up → sample project → first
real project) and the teammate journey (invitation → assigned task). Fidelity was mid-level on
purpose: real enough to test comprehension, rough enough that participants critiqued the flow
instead of the colours.

## Testing

Five moderated remote sessions per audience, task-based: _"You're evaluating this tool for your
agency — decide whether you'd shortlist it."_ We measured task success, time to first project
view, and asked participants to narrate what they believed the product did at three checkpoints.

## Iterations

Testing produced two significant revisions:

1. The sample project initially opened on a **board view** — evaluators from non-agile teams
   found it alien. The fix: the use-case question now also selects the default view (board,
   list, or timeline).
2. The "start your own project" call-to-action was too quiet; several participants stayed in
   the sample longer than useful. It became a primary action anchored to the sample banner.

## Final solution

The shipped flow: two questions, a themed sample project as the landing experience, contextual
setup prompts that appear where their answers take effect, and a separate short path for invited
teammates that lands them directly on their assigned work.

## Design-system considerations

The redesign added three patterns to the product's design system rather than one-off screens: a
progressive-setup panel, a sample-content banner, and an empty-state template with a built-in
primary action slot. Each shipped with usage documentation so future features could reuse them.

## Accessibility considerations

- The setup panel and banner are native landmarks with correct heading structure, keyboard
  operability, and visible focus.
- The onboarding flow was tested with keyboard-only navigation and a screen reader pass;
  progress announcements use a polite live region.
- All new components met AA contrast in both product themes.

## Developer handoff

Handoff was a living Figma spec: flows annotated with state logic, component references into the
existing system, and a written decision log explaining _why_ each screen exists (which
prevented well-meaning "simplifications" from undoing the research). I stayed embedded through
the build sprint, reviewing staging builds against the spec.

## Lessons learned

- **Prototype the stakeholder's idea too.** Testing the tour overlay cost three days and bought
  total alignment — cheaper than weeks of debate.
- **Empty states are onboarding.** The wizard got all the attention historically, but the blank
  dashboard did the real damage.
- **Progressive setup needs an owner.** Deferring questions works only if something tracks and
  resurfaces them; the setup panel earned its complexity.

## What I'd do next

With more time: instrument the sample project's "graduation" moment properly, test a variant
that imports real data from the user's previous tool, and extend the role-aware logic to email
onboarding, which still sends every user an identical sequence.
