The gap between "we did research" and "the design changed because of it" is where most research
value dies. Findings decks get presented, heads nod, and then design proceeds from the same
assumptions as before. The problem usually isn't the research — it's that findings and
decisions are different species, and nobody owns the translation. This article is a working
method for that translation.

## Outline

### 1. Why findings don't become decisions on their own

- Findings describe the world; decisions commit to a response
- The "interesting but inert" deck problem

### 2. Write findings as forces, not facts

- From "users didn't notice the save button" to "users need confirmation their work is safe"
- A finding worth keeping implies a pressure on the design

### 3. The translation table

- Columns: finding → implication → design response → confidence → cost
- Working through one real (anonymised) example end to end
- Where multiple findings collide: making the conflict explicit instead of averaging it

### 4. Confidence-weighting: not all findings deserve equal force

- Sample, method, and consistency as confidence inputs
- When to design for a low-confidence finding anyway (cheap response, high downside)

### 5. Closing the loop

- Annotating designs with the finding that motivated each decision
- What this does for stakeholder reviews (arguments become traceable)
